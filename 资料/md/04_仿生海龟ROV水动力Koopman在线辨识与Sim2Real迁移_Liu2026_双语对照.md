INTRODUCTION B IO-INSPIRED underwater robots (BURs), leveraging their exceptional biological compatibility [1] and low environmental disturbance characteristics [2], have become in- dispensable tools in ﬁelds such as marine environmental moni- toring [3] and archaeological exploration [4]. Compared to tradi- tional propeller driven autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) and remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), BURs generate signif- icantly lower noise [5], hydrodynamic wake [6], and sediment resuspension during locomotion [7]. For instance, Katzschmann et al. successfully utilized acoustic communication to control a robotic ﬁsh for close observation of marine life, providing an effective platform for studying organisms in their natural habitats [8]. However, the unique morphologies [9], numerous control degrees of freedom [10], and diverse bio-inspired propulsion mechanisms of these robots [11] pose signiﬁcant challenges for precise dynamic modeling. To address this, researchers have proposed various methods [12]. Wang et al. established a three dimensional motion model for a robotic ﬁsh, including helical motion, based on Euler’s equations of motion and grey box model hydrodynamic estimation [13]. Nonetheless, these methods typically rely on physical prior knowledge, including speciﬁcrobot conﬁgurations, anddeterminingmodel parameters often requires extensive time for meticulous design and exper- imental calibration. In previous work [14], although the single rigid body dynamic equations were employed to describe the motion characteristics of the amphibious turtle robot, the model still struggles to adequately capture the dynamic features arising from the three dimensional oscillation of the ﬂippers. Fortunately, data driven approaches, such as deep learning [15], sparse identiﬁcation of nonlinear dynamics (SINDy) [16], and the Koopman operator [17], offer promising solutions for identifying nonlinear system behaviors solely from motion data. Deep learning involves solving nonlinear optimization problems and may suffer from convergence issues [15], while SINDy re- quires pre-constructing a dictionary of candidate functions [16], which can be restrictive for BURs with limited prior knowledge. Incontrast,theKoopmanoperatorframeworkgloballylinearizes nonlinear systems by lifting them into an inﬁnite dimensional linear space while preserving control information. Koopman based system identiﬁcation has been successfully applied to various systems, including spherical robots [18], soft contin- uum manipulators [19], and wheeled robots [20]. Despite reducing reliance on physical prior knowledge, Koopman-based methods still require substantial datasets for training [22]. Acquiring sufﬁcient stable motion data for BURs is often challenging due to experimental constraints. A feasible strategy is to use dynamic simulators to generate diverse mo- tion data for prior model identiﬁcation, which is then updated and corrected with a small amount of real-world experimen- tal data, enabling a Simulation-to-Reality (Sim2Real) transfer. This strategy has been widely adopted in embodied AI, in- cluding legged and humanoid robotics [23]. For example, the ﬁne grained medium model proposed by Choi et al. enabled a reinforcement learning agent to achieve good adaptation to unfamiliar environments, overcoming limitations of the training data distribution [24]. Furthermore, BURs face the challenge of time varying dy- namics in practical applications: factors such as varying pay- loads [18], mechanical wear of components [13], and coupling among multiple controllers [12] can lead to drift in dynamic parameters. Therefore, developing robust identiﬁcation algo- rithms capable of online tracking of model variations is crucial. For instance, addressing the online identiﬁcation of soft envi- ronmental dynamic models, Schindeler et al. proposed a two stage polynomial linearized Hunt-Crossley model identiﬁcation method, achieving high precision online identiﬁcation of nonlin- ear contact dynamics via a look up table recursive least squares estimator [25].
